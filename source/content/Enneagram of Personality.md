## The 9 Types in Depth

The Enneagram in itself is a tool for personal growth and goes beyond classical typology theory. It’s a system of guiding motives and basic fears. The core types can be seen as its personality types. Each type also has a “wing” - which serves to further narrow down the types. It’s always a neighboring type that lends it’s properties to the main type.

# The Basics

The Enneagram includes a variety of subtopics, and we will briefly examine the most significant among them.

## Triads

The nine types of the Enneagram are divided among Heart, Head and Body types:

- **Heart types** depend on their _emotional_ intelligence, to understand their own reactions and connect with others.
- **Head types** depend on their _intellectual_ intelligence, to make sense of things and navigate the world around them.
- **Body types** depend on their _instinctual_ intelligence, to follow their gut and respond to threats and opportunities.

The “rule of three” can be found a lot inside the Enneagram, meaning that type is directly connected to two other types in some way - forming different forms of groups.

Beyond the classic Heart, Head and Body triads, the Enneagram also connects to other psychological groupings that provide additional layers of understanding - complementing the triads by offering different perspectives on motivation, behavior and emotional response within the Enneagram framework.

### Horney’s Coping Strategies

These describe how types relate to others through patterns of moving _toward_ (**compliance**), _against_ (**aggression**) or _away_ (**withdrawal**) from people. This model highlights fundamental interpersonal strategies underlying each type.

### Harmony Types

This grouping focuses on how types respond emotionally, especially under stress or conflict, dividing them into categories like **positive outlook**, **competency** or **reactivity** to better understand coping mechanisms.

### Object Relations Groups

Rooted in psychodynamic theory, these categorize types based on early relational patterns and attachment styles, shedding light on how individuals internalize relationships and form self-image.

## Integration & Disintegration

Each basic type in the Enneagram is connected by two lines to another type. There are two trains of thought on how this effect occurs.

1. One line connects to the type which the person has left behind or repressed in childhood, while the second line connects to the type which the person may grow into
2. When we are at our best, we integrate into a “higher” type, whereas we go to a “lower” type when we’re under stress

In both cases the connecting lines highlight how each type can possess indispensable strengths, but at the same time has darker sides that are full of challenges. The inclusion of these lines moves the Enneagram from a purely descriptive personality model to one that is dynamic.

## Wings

Each Enneagram type is flanked by two neighboring types, which are known as its **wings**. The dominant type may be influenced by one of these adjacent types, resulting in a more nuanced personality expression.

<div>
    <img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/SnakeFist007/puzzle-of-personality/refs/heads/main/source/assets/wings.svg" width="300" />
</div>

Wings are not separate types, but rather variations that shape how the core motivations and fears are expressed. While most people tend to lean toward one wing more strongly, it's possible to draw traits from both, or experience a shift in wing influence over time.

For example, a Type 4 may have a 3 wing (`4w3`) or a 5 wing (`4w5`), each adding different traits to the core type.

## Instinctual Variants

Within the Enneagram framework, there are two distinct concepts commonly associated with the term _“instincts”_. Despite their similar terminology, these refer to two entirely different models, each with its own internal logic, typing method, and purpose. Understanding this distinction is essential to avoid confusion.

### Stacking

This model was developed by Don Riso and Russ Hudson and functions as a separate two-dimensional typing system that interacts with – but is not subordinate to – the Enneagram type itself.

It is based on how individuals prioritize three instinctual drives: _self-preservation (sp)_, _social (so)_ and _sexual (sx)_.
The result is an **instinctual stacking**, such as `sp/so` or `sx/sp`, where the third instinct is typically considered the _”blind spot”_.

Crucially, this is not a subtype system in the strict sense. A designation like sp7 does not mean that sp is a subtype of [[Enneagram of Personality/Type 7 - The Adventurer|Type 7]]. Rather, it is a shorthand for someone who has a self-preservation dominant instinctual stacking (e.g. sp/so) in combination with being a [[Enneagram of Personality/Type 7 - The Adventurer|Type 7]]. The traits of this combination emerge from the interaction between two separate systems: Enneagram type and instinctual stacking.

This approach does not use countertypes, since every variation results from the unique interaction of type and instinctual emphasis. It is commonly used alongside the **Levels of Development**.

Within the realms of this guide, I will use this approach in the deep dives into each type.

### Subtypes

This (older) model was developed by Claudio Naranjo. Here, the instincts (sp, so, sx) are not treated as independent elements, but rather as **subtypes within each Enneagram type**. Each type is divided into three variants – in the case of [[Enneagram of Personality/Type 5 - The Investigator|Type 5]] we have for example: `sp5`, `so5` & `sx5`.

Here the term “instinct” is more metaphorical. There is no independent definition or structure to the instincts themselves – no standalone instinct types, stackings, or blind spots. An `sp5` is simply a subtype of [[Enneagram of Personality/Type 5 - The Investigator|Type 5]] and does not imply a separable “self-preservation instinct” layered onto the type.

As such, there's no such thing as a “shared” social instinct across different types (e.g. `so2` vs. `so3`). Any thematic similarity is incidental and does not arise from a unified instinctual category.

This model also does not use stacking systems like `sx/sp` or `so/sp`. Instead, it incorporates the concept of **countertypes** to explain subtype expressions that appear atypical for a given Enneagram type. It typically does **not** include the Levels of Development as a formal component either.
